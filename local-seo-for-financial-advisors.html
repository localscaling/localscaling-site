Why financial advice is different

Nobody hires an advisor on the first visit

Most people go looking for an advisor after something changes. A retirement date, an inheritance, a divorce, a business sale, a new job with a pile of stock. They search with real money on the line, and they are careful about who they call.

So they read. They look at how you are paid, who you work with, what a first meeting looks like, and whether you seem like someone they could trust for twenty years. A thin site with a stock photo of a sunset gives them nothing to go on.

The map shows them you are close. Your pages show them you are the right person.

Rules shape all of it. What you can say, how you can use reviews, and who controls your listing depend on how you are registered and which firm you work through. Some advisors at large firms cannot touch their own website. Every word we write goes to your compliance team before it goes live.

Two spots on one page

The map gets the call. The listing under it closes the deal.

When someone searches for a financial advisor, Google shows the map first and the regular results under it. Very few people hire an advisor from the map alone. They use it to see who is close, then read the sites under it to learn how each firm works, how it is paid, and who they would sit across from. A practice that holds a spot in both gets the first meeting.

Where the calls go missing

Six things we find on most advisor profiles

None of them look urgent. Together they send the careful reader to the firm across town.

  1. A category that says nothingMany practices sit under a broad finance category, or a category picked by whoever set up the profile years ago. Financial planner and Financial consultant tell Google what you do.
  2. Old listings from a former firmAdvisors who change firms or go independent often leave a profile and a dozen directory listings under the old name. Those keep collecting searches and sending them nowhere.
  3. One page for everythingRetirement planning, investment management, tax planning, and estate coordination all on one services page. Google picks one page per search, and that page is a poor match for all of them.
  4. Nothing about how you are paidFee only, fee based, flat fee, or a percentage of assets. People search for these words, and a site that never says which one you are loses them.
  5. No facesPeople want to see the advisor they would hire. A site with no team photos and no plain bios makes a careful reader wonder who they would actually be talking to.
  6. Reviews nobody thought aboutSome firms never ask, and some ask with no plan for compliance. Both leave the profile thin. A clear, approved process fixes it.

Questions

What advisory firms ask us

Are we allowed to ask clients for Google reviews?

Often yes, with conditions that depend on how you are registered and what your firm allows. Your compliance team decides how we ask, what replies say, and whether reviews appear on your site. We never offer anything in return for a review.

How long until we get more calls?

Usually three to six months to start seeing big results. Fixing the profile and listings moves the map first. Planning pages and town pages take longer and keep building.

Our firm controls our website. Can we still do this?

Usually some of it. Many firms let advisors run their own Google profile, and some allow local pages or a separate site. We start from your firm's marketing rules and ask before we change anything.

We just went independent. What happens to the old listings?

We find the old profile and the directory listings with your former firm's name or number, then update or remove them carefully. Some of them may belong to the old firm, and we tell you which before anything changes.

Most of our meetings are on video. Does local search still matter?

Yes. People still search for an advisor near them, and many want the option of meeting in person. Your profile wins nearby searches, and your planning pages reach people further out.

Should we list our fees?

Only if you want to. Some firms show a starting fee or how fees are worked out, and others explain it on a call. People search for how advisors are paid, so a plain page on it helps either way.

Who approves what goes on the site?

You and your compliance team, every time. We write in plain words about what you do and who you help, with no talk of returns, and nothing goes live until it is approved.
