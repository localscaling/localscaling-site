Why orthodontics is different

Two very different people search for you

Most of your patients start with a parent. A dentist mentions crowding, or a friend's child gets braces, and the parent searches that night. The American Association of Orthodontists suggests a child's first orthodontic check no later than age seven, so some of those parents are looking well before treatment starts.

The other patient is an adult. They have thought about straightening their teeth for years, and now they compare clear aligners, braces, and cost. They read more, they ask more questions, and they often pay for it themselves.

Treatment can run for a long time. Parents want to like the office before they sign up.

Your competition is wider than the orthodontist down the road. General dentists offer aligners, and some aligners are sold by mail. The map and a clear page about specialty training, the consult, and payment plans are where a specialist makes the case for a visit.

Two spots on one page

The map gets the call. The listing under it closes the deal.

When someone searches for an orthodontist, Google shows the map first and the regular results under it. Parents use the map to see who is close to home and school, then read the reviews. Adults thinking about aligners read the sites under it to learn about cost, time, and what treatment looks like. A practice that holds a spot in both gets the consult.

We work both. Your Google Business Profile wins the map. Your website wins the listing under it. The audit checks where you stand in each.

Where the calls go missing

Six things we find on most orthodontic practice profiles

Each one looks minor. Together they send the parent to the practice across town.

  1. Listed as a dentistSome practices sit under Dentist or Dental clinic. Orthodontist is its own category, and it is the one that matches the search.
  2. Satellite offices with the wrong hoursMany practices see patients in a second office a few days a week. If the profile says it is open every day, people call to an empty office and Google gets mixed signals.
  3. The doctor and the practice split apartA profile for the orthodontist and another for the practice, with different phone numbers. Reviews land on both, and neither gets strong.
  4. One page for all treatmentBraces, clear aligners, early treatment, and retainers on a single page. Google picks one page to show for a search, and a page about everything is a weak match for each one.
  5. Nothing about cost or paymentCost is the first question most families ask. A site that never mentions consults, insurance, or monthly payment plans sends them to the practice that does.
  6. Reviews asked for once, at the endTreatment runs for months, and many practices only ask on the day braces come off. Good moments come earlier too, and a steady flow of reviews does more than a burst.

Questions

What orthodontic practices ask us

Most of our patients come from dentist referrals. Do we need this?

Referrals still matter, and many referred parents search your name before they call. Your profile and reviews decide whether they book with you or with someone closer. Local search also brings families who never had a referral.

How long until we get more consults?

Usually three to six months to start seeing big results. Fixing the profile and listings moves the map first. Treatment pages and town pages take longer and keep building.

We have satellite offices open two days a week. Can each one have a profile?

Yes, if the office is staffed during the hours it lists. Each one gets true hours, its own phone number if it has one, and its own page. An office with no staff should never be listed.

General dentists near us offer Invisalign. Can we still win those searches?

Often, yes. Orthodontist is its own category, and your pages can explain what a specialist does and who treatment is right for. The map still weighs distance, so the towns closest to you come first.

We already have a dental marketing company. Is this any different?

Dental marketing usually targets cleanings and checkups. Orthodontic searches are fewer, come from parents and adults weighing a long treatment, and need their own category, pages, and review timing. We build for those searches only.

Can we post before and after photos?

Only with written consent from the patient, or from a parent for a child. Photos of the office and the team do a lot of the work too.

Should we list prices?

That is up to you. Many practices explain what changes the cost, what the consult covers, and whether they offer payment plans. People search for cost, so a plain page on it helps either way.

Do you write about treatment on our site?

We write treatment pages and town pages in plain words. You approve anything clinical before it publishes, and we make no claims about outcomes or finish dates.
