Why chiropractic is different

Most new patients are strangers to chiropractic

Plenty of people searching for a chiropractor have never been to one. They are in pain, a little nervous, and unsure what happens at a first visit or what it will cost. They read reviews for whether the doctor listened, explained things, and did not push a long plan on the first day.

Then there is the patient who already knows what they want. A sports chiropractor, someone trained in prenatal care, a practice that takes their insurance, or one technique they trust. Those searches are specific, fewer practices compete for them, and a page written for each one is what wins them.

A patient who trusts you comes back for years and sends their family. The first visit is the hard part.

The trust works both ways. Google reads health claims carefully, and so do patients. A practice that promises to cure everything reads as a sales pitch to both. Plain pages about what you treat and how you work earn more trust than big promises.

Two spots on one page

When someone searches for a chiropractor, Google shows the map first and the regular results under it. The person in pain today calls from the map. A practice that holds a spot in both gets both patients.

Where the calls go missing

Six things we find on most chiropractic profiles

None of them look serious alone. Together they send new patients to the practice down the road.

  1. Categories that do not exist or do not fitSome guides tell practices to add categories Google does not offer, or ones for services nobody at the practice provides. Chiropractor is the primary. Anything else has to match real work done in your office.
  2. One page called ServicesAdjustments, sciatica, headaches, prenatal care, and auto injuries on one page. Google ranks whole pages, so a page about everything ranks for nothing.
  3. A shared office with a muddled listingMany practices share space with a massage therapist or a physical therapist. When the listings blur together, Google cannot tell which business is which, and both rank worse.
  4. An old name or address on the directoriesThe practice moved, changed names, or an associate bought it, and the old details still sit on half the listings Google checks. The mismatch drags the profile down.
  5. No word about insurance or costNew patients want to know if you take their plan or what a first visit costs. A profile and site that say nothing send them to the practice that does.
  6. Review replies that say too muchA reply that mentions someone's treatment confirms they are a patient. Short, kind replies with no details are safer and read better to the next person.

Questions

What chiropractors ask us

We are a cash practice. Does that change anything?

A little. Patients still search the same way, but the questions change. Your pages explain what a visit costs and why you work that way, so the people who call are the ones who are fine with it.

How long until we see new patients?

Usually three to six months to start seeing big results. Fixing the profile and listings moves the map first. Service pages and reviews take longer and keep building.

We share an office with a massage therapist. Do we need separate listings?

If you are separate businesses, yes, each with its own name, phone, and profile. If the massage therapist works for your practice, it is one listing, and massage goes on as a service or a secondary category.

Should each of our doctors have a Google profile?

Sometimes. Google allows one for a practitioner who sees patients, and it has rules for how it is named. Set up right it can help. Set up wrong it competes with the practice. We decide that with you.

Can we get more of one kind of patient?

Partly. The pages and categories decide which searches you win, so we build more around sports care or prenatal care if that is the work you want. Other patients still come, and some of them turn into the patients you were after.

Can we compete with the big chiropractic chains?

On the map, yes. A chain location is one profile like yours, and Google ranks the closest, best reviewed, best described practice. You win your own neighborhoods and the specialty searches the chains do not bother with.

Do you write about health on our site?

We write service pages and city pages in plain words. You approve anything clinical before it publishes, and we make no claims about outcomes.
